# Lessons

What we tried with the evolved cell rule, what worked, what didn't and why, so settled questions aren't reopened. The lessons from the hand-designed system (excitable medium, gates, hand-made learning rules) are in `old_manual_gates/LESSONS.md`.

## Principles carried over from the hand-designed system

- **Local behaviour only, no global god.** No global clock, no broadcast signals, no harness switching the cells' behaviour. The only things from outside are the body's inputs: sensor pulses and taste.
- **No hidden scale.** Cells must not know the sheet's size or their position in it; only their own state and their neighbours'.
- **Inputs spike.** Sparse kicks, never continuous levels.
- **Stochastic updates only (the golden rule).** Every cell updates at random times, never all in lockstep, and every rule must survive some noise in its cells' numbers. Lockstep caused artefacts twice: the robot's first evolved sheet flipped every cell every tick and steered by the phase ("Discrete time invites tricks"), and in `paths.py` poisoning set the distance map flickering in a checkerboard. The lizard NCA updates each cell with probability 0.5 for the same reason. The robot's `cell.py` still updates in lockstep and must change before more robot work.
- **Random inputs, nothing crafted.** Learning must work with events arriving in random order, not a training sequence designed to make it work.
- **Excitable media percolate.** In a connected threshold medium, a pulse either dies or floods everything, and tuning the threshold only moves the boundary between the two. A flood fires every actuator at once, so their pushes cancel.
- **Reward needs variability.** Deterministic trials give the same outcome every time, so reward has nothing to select between.
- **No god in the creature's learning; any help in finding the creature.** The rule against a global god applies to how the creature learns within its life: only local cells and its body's inputs. Finding such a creature may use anything: hand design, curricula, stages that add variables, seeding. Whether such a creature could have evolved without help is a separate, later question. The first goal is a creature that learns, and is as simple as it can be.

## Evolving the cell rule (cell.py, evolve.py, mapelites.py)

- **The rule is a polynomial per cell variable, found by evolution;** the aim is still a rule that can be said in a sentence or two.
- **The body can give the answer away.** With each actuator between its own sensor's inputs and pulling towards its own side, "copy your neighbour" is already a line-follower: only the rim was used. Actuators are now thrusters (they push away from their side), so approach needs signals to cross the disc.
- **Discrete time invites tricks.** With whole-step updates, the evolved sheet flipped every cell between −1 and +1 each tick and steered by phase differences. A rate per variable (0.1) makes the rule an equation of motion: firing became a real excursion and recovery, and activity sparse (6% of cells above firing level while approaching).
- **Move and approach evolve in seconds to minutes with CMA-ES; learning didn't.** Choose and taste stayed on the "eat everything" plateau (50% food in both halves of lives), whether taste landed in the middle or at the point of contact, with or without the slow variable m.
  - Scoring the worse of a red-food and a blue-food life made standing still (0) beat eating everything, so poison costs half, as a stepping stone.
  - The approach rule sits on a narrow ridge: at a search spread of 0.1 its neighbours die and the search collapses to doing nothing. From scratch it collapses too.
  - CMA-ES needs no gradient, but it still moves only towards improvements it samples; on a plateau its rankings are decided by noise.
- **A wide search found learning.** MAP-Elites keeps the best rule for each kind of behaviour (meals per life, and how eating food and eating poison change during a life), with the two taste strengths as body genes. Within about 1.5 hours it found a rule that learns the taste task: on 128 fresh worlds, the one block is eaten 1.23 then 1.70 times per half-life when it is food, and 1.04 then 0.16 when it is poison. After a bad taste it keeps away from the block (about 21 units, against 13 when it is food), rather than freezing.
- **Fixed evaluation worlds let flukes in.** The first "learner" (+0.48 rise in food share on 16 worlds) scored 0 on fresh ones: it ate everything early and then stopped. Learning now only counts when both halves of lives have meals, and the best learner is re-checked on fresh worlds.
- **Where the learner keeps its memory: in w, in the taste cells.** After a bad taste, the taste cell that was kicked lowers its w and holds it for the rest of the life; nothing else differs between food and poison lives. So the memory is local, where the taste arrived, and a side the robot hasn't bitten from hasn't learned.
- **m wasn't used as memory or as a cell type.** It settles to about −0.47 in every cell within 200 ticks: a constant offset, which through the m·x terms shifts every coefficient alike. No differentiation into kinds of cell appeared; nothing asked for it.
- **A taste does leave a lasting difference in a sheet that hasn't learned, but it's chaotic** (a changed oscillation), not a usable memory.
- **The evolved rules depend on the tick size, so they aren't continuous-time systems.** With a time step dt (rates are per unit time, pulses a Poisson process), the approach rule and the learner work only at the dt they were evolved at (1). At dt 0.5 the sheet goes quiet (no thruster firing at all); at dt 2 approach eats 1.55 blocks instead of 2.6, and the learner barely eats. Their activity comes from overshoot at the coarse step, a subtler version of the flip-every-tick trick. Rules have to be evolved across tick sizes to rule this out.
- **Tick-robust learning needed taste on its own channel.** With taste as a signed kick to v (the same variable sensor pulses kick), about 3 hours of search across tick sizes found no learning that survived fresh worlds. With a fourth variable t that only tastes kick (fast, like a neuromodulator rather than a spike), MAP-Elites found one within 90 minutes: on fresh worlds poison meals fall about 90% after the first bite, at every tick size from 0.25 to 1 (food also falls, by about 35–40%). Evolution put taste almost entirely at the point of contact (strength 1.1), almost none at the centre.
- **It steers with a travelling wave, like the activation front across heart muscle.** Watched live, the learner (and the approach rule it descends from) sends waves of activation sweeping across the whole disc, and steers by changing their direction and timing, so one side's thrusters fire before the other's. Every cell takes part: v is above the firing level in 18% of cells at any moment, and each cell spikes about every 18 time units. An interesting, efficient solution (a global oscillation does the routing), but not sparse; nothing in the score discouraged it, as only thruster firings cost anything. So activity now costs: each cell's spikes, above a free rate of one per 100 time units.
- **Activity costs, and the robust learners got sparser.** With each cell's spikes above one per 100 time units costing score, MAP-Elites (seeded with the dense learner) found learners at about 2 spikes per cell per 100 time units instead of 5.7. The first ones learned at dt 1 and 0.5 but went quiet at 0.25, so their sparsity again leaned on the step size; validation at 0.25 caught them. After about 2.7 hours it found one that learns at all three tick sizes on fresh worlds (+0.27 rise in food share, 2.6 spikes per cell per 100), kept as `kernels/grid12/sparse_robust_learner.json`. It still steers with travelling waves, just thinner ones.
- **What the taste learners learn is a switch, not an association.** One bite sets a lasting state (lower w in the taste cell on the side that bit) that makes the robot less drawn to blocks; after food it carries on. In the taste world (one block) that looks like "avoid that thing", but in forage it eats less of both colours: closest to sensitisation (a general change in responsiveness after a bad experience), not associative learning (red means poison, blue still food).
- **Choose: nothing in one night.** MAP-Elites on choose (one red and one blue block), seeded with the taste archive: 287,000 candidates in 8 hours (with racing, 3% went on to the second tick size), 199 niches, no learner that held up on fresh worlds (best −0.07; +0.21 on the search's own worlds was a fluke).
- **We had been asking for one-shot learning by accident.** Taste and choose lives held 1–3 meals, so each colour was usually tasted once at most, and scoring the whole life gave a rule that learns gradually almost no credit (most meals come before it has learned). A fair test needs tens of tastes of each colour per life, scored on how the life ends and how much better it gets, while staying far from the thousands of trials of deep learning. Simply adding blocks didn't add meals: with blocks seen on every side, thrusters push against each other and the robot stalls. Hence the next stages: graze (a cluttered world, both colours food, score meals) and discriminate (long lives, score the last third and the improvement).
- **Scoring at dt 1 and 0.5 wasn't enough either.** Graze (from the robust approach rule) quickly reached 13–16 meals per life at dt 1 and 0.5 while collapsing to 2–5 at 0.25: evolution exploits whatever the finest step it is scored at is. Scoring at 1, 0.5 and 0.25 (raced in stages, so most candidates only ever live at dt 1) and validating at 0.125 keeps it honest: the validation held at 11–14 meals at all four.
- **Nothing asks for spiking, and nothing asks for simplicity.** Inside the cell the rule is a general smooth equation. Spiking is pushed only from outside: inputs are pulses (by principle), and a thruster fires only when its v rises through 0.5, so to keep pushing a thruster cell has to keep going up and down. The "activity" cost counts those crossings, not activity: a cell held at v = 0.9 all its life costs nothing, so it penalises oscillation rather than rewarding quiet. The only pressure towards a simple rule is the coefficient penalty, and at 0.001 (needed so approach doesn't go quiet on 24×24) it's almost nothing, so no rule has come out sparse. If simplicity is the aim, the levers are pruning a working rule (zero terms while the score holds) and, if oscillation itself isn't wanted, a thruster that pushes in proportion to how far v is above 0.5.
- **The curriculum carries one lineage.** Each CMA-ES stage hands one rule to the next (`--from`), and discriminate was seeded with that one graze rule, so learning could only be built on one particular forager, chosen for eating, not for being teachable (a busy sheet, 12–17 spikes per cell per 100 time units, and chaotic: its score swings with float rounding). `--seed` can take several kernels or a whole archive, so several different foragers could be carried instead.

## Cells, variables and meta-structures

- **There's a trade-off between complexity per cell and complexity in the arrangement of cells.** Rule 110, a 1-D automaton, does things that look topologically impossible in 1-D (two signals crossing) because its information lives in gliders, meta-blocks of several cells that can pass through or transform each other: the meta-level has an extra dimension. Variables per cell are another way to add one: each variable is a layer over the sheet, and a pulse in one layer could pass under a pulse in another.
- **The two cost differently.** Variables grow the rule quadratically (about (2K)²/2 products per variable), so search gets harder and rules harder to say; a bigger sheet leaves the rule alone and only costs compute per cell. Prefer simplicity over efficiency: a solution that works with many cells and few variables could later be translated into fewer cells with more variables, if that suited a particular medium.
- **Meta-structures need a rule that supports them** (gliders, solitons; "edge of chaos" dynamics), room around the sensors and thrusters for them to form, and time for them to act. In a plain excitable medium colliding pulses annihilate (each meets the other's refractory wake), which is why the hand-designed medium couldn't cross routes.
- **Directional inputs aren't needed for particles.** Rule 110 is asymmetric, but Life is symmetric and totalistic: a cell only counts its live neighbours, much as ours takes their mean. Gliders arise from patterns spread over several cells, not from a cell sensing direction. So the mean-input cell may already be enough.
- **The sparse learner has no travelling pulses to collide.** Its sheet is never still (321 spontaneous spikes in 150 ticks with no input), and its "pulse trains" are that rhythm. A sensor kick doesn't launch a pulse: it locally disrupts the rhythm (kicking a west input removes about 39 spikes in the west third and changes nothing elsewhere), and two kicks on opposite sides just add (−90 against −39 − 50). So no glider-like structures yet. Still to check: once graze and discriminate work at 12×12, does a bigger sheet (around 20×20, sensors and thrusters spaced out) do better?
## The robot curriculum: growing the sheet, then adding variables

- **Why.** On the 12×12 sheet, graze evolved well: 17–21 meals per 2,000 time units at every tick size, and about 55 tastes per discriminate life. But MAP-Elites on discriminate stalled: after 2 hours, 88 niches, and no rule that changed how much it ate of either colour. One reason is specific. A sensor group's taste cell sat between its red and blue inputs and saw only their mean, so "red fired" and "blue fired" looked the same where the taste arrived. A colour association would have needed the taste signal to spread to cells touching only one input and combine there, two steps with nothing rewarding the first. More generally, six sensor groups were crammed onto 112 cells: no room for structures (meta-blocks, "Cells, variables and meta-structures") that could carry direction or tell inputs apart. Rather than make the cell more complex (directional inputs), we made the sheet bigger: same rule, more room, simplest first.
- **What changed.** The brain is 24×24 (448 cells) in the same robot and world. Each sensor group is spread out: inputs 3 cells either side, the taste cell by the rim, the thruster 3 cells in. The 12×12 body also turned out to have a rounding fault (Python rounds halves to even): sensor group 0's thruster and taste cell were one cell, and the body was lopsided. Positions now sit just inside half-cell points, and the body checks that its special cells are distinct. Each tick costs about 4× (cost is per cell). The curriculum restarted from scratch; the 12×12 rules are kept in `kernels/grid12/` but don't work in the new body.
- **Penalties tuned on the small sheet bit on the big one.** The activity cost is per cell (4× the cells), and the 24×24 move rule had large coefficients (total 143), so both the activity cost and the sparsity penalty (0.01 per unit) outweighed anything approach could earn: twice the search shrank the rule and went quiet instead of approaching. Activity is now free in move and approach, and approach runs with sparsity 0.001. The same happened in discriminate, which scores only the last third of a life but charged activity over all of it: the 24×24 graze rule earned 8.25 from its last third (17.5 food, 18.5 poison) and paid 7.7 for the whole life's thruster firings and spikes, so any rule that kept moving scored about nothing. Activity is now charged over the scored third. Discriminate also lives each candidate in 16 worlds, not 32 (about 100 meals per life is less noisy), which halves the cost.
- **The sheet builds circuits.** Watched live from a fresh life (near-blank noise, ±0.1 in every variable), an early approach-stage rule organises the sheet within a few dozen ticks into lasting structure that looks like wires and junctions, where the 12×12 sheet only ever showed a uniform buzz. Restarted from other noise it comes back very similar but not identical: self-organisation, like development, rather than a fixed pattern. Probably anchored by the body's special cells (the only cells that get kicks) and the edge of the disc. To find out: how much it varies across seeds, whether it settles, which variables form the wires, and whether signals travel along them from sensors to thrusters.
- **Stripes one cell wide.** Early rules often make alternate rows active and inactive: the finest pattern the grid allows, favoured by the neighbour mean under lateral inhibition (in stripes 6 of a cell's 8 neighbours are opposite to it, in a checkerboard 4). The spatial counterpart of the tick artefact, but not necessarily wrong: lateral inhibition makes alternating cell fates in real tissue (Notch–Delta). If it persists and gets in the way, test rules on another lattice (hexagonal), as we test them at other tick sizes.
- **24×24 didn't find colour learning either.** Graze on 24×24 reached 32 meals per 2,000 time units (twice the 12×12 best). Discriminate ran 10 hours: 33,600 candidates, 179 niches (the 12×12 run stalled at 88), best score 3.51 from a dense forager eating about 190 meals per life of both colours, and no learner that held up on fresh worlds (best −0.05).
- **More cells rather than smarter cells: 48×48.** The next step could have been cells that sense each variable's gradient (east minus west, south minus north): 16 inputs, 612 coefficients, about 3× the cost per tick, and a shared compass. We chose a bigger sheet instead: 48×48 (1,804 cells), the same symmetric 180-coefficient cell, about 4× the cost per tick. Identical, direction-blind cells can still hold direction in their patterns: the stripes come from symmetry breaking (a Turing-like instability: the even state can't last, the noise and the boundaries choose the layout), and a stripe, like a glider, has an orientation no single cell has. A bigger sheet gives such meta-structures room to form; the hand-built XOR gate alone needed about 32×32. Sensor groups are scaled with the sheet (inputs 6 cells either side, thrusters 6 cells in). To keep the curriculum to hours, CMA-ES candidates live in 8 worlds (not 16), validation uses 32, approach runs 250 generations and graze 150.
- **48×48 was worse, and no sparser.** With the same budget-trimmed curriculum (8 worlds per candidate; approach 250 generations, graze 150), approach reached 1.24 (24×24: 2.7) and graze 16 meals per 2,000 time units (24×24: 32), with about 10 spikes per cell per 100 time units (24×24: about 12). The activity cost in graze didn't turn the stripes into narrow paths in a sea of inactivity, and no new structure appeared. Signals have twice as far to cross, and the search had less budget. Back to 24×24; a much bigger sheet (96×96) would need the GPU version to be affordable. The 48×48 rules are kept in `kernels/grid48/`.
- **Taste now arrives at the input cells.** The bare-sheet experiments showed taste can't be carried far, and that colour-specific learning needs it next to where the colour signals enter. So the separate taste cell is gone: a bite kicks t in the facing group's red and blue input cells (and, by the body gene, the centre). Graze doesn't depend on it (the 24×24 graze rule still eats 32.8 meals per 2,000 time units).
- **Two variables (v, w) can move but hardly forage.** The complexification curriculum's first stages used v and w only (30 coefficients, `--variables v,w`) on 24×24: move reached 8.1 (as good as four variables), but approach only 0.46 (four variables: 2.7) and graze 1.4 meals per 2,000 time units (four variables: 32), its best at generation 7 of 150 and nothing better after. A robot that barely eats gets almost no tastes, so it's no base for learning. Kept in `kernels/vw_only/`.
- **So learning gets its own variables on top of the full forager.** The cell now has six variables (e fast, z slow, both free for learning, e.g. an eligibility trace and a gate) and cubic terms in a cell's own variables (a three-factor rule such as e·t·z is one term): 147 terms per variable, 882 coefficients. (In graze each tick looked about 5× the cost; in discriminate it was far worse, traced below.) Old rules load with the new coefficients at zero and behave as before (the states differ only by float rounding). Discriminate then searches only the terms involving e or z (622), with the 24×24 four-variable forager frozen (`--freeze v,w,m,t`).
- **The first six-variable night searched almost nothing (aborted after 9 hours, 1,280 candidates; last time 33,600).** Two causes, both traced. (1) Speed: the cell written out as one big loop body over lives stopped vectorising: 263 ns per cell per life against the four-variable cell's 14 (19×; in graze, with 8 lives side by side rather than 32, it had looked like 5×). Written as one loop per variable, with that variable's coefficients read before it, it vectorises again: 36 ns, and a whole discriminate candidate 2.2× the old cost. (2) Most candidates wrecked the forager: with 622 free terms, random e and z terms at spread 0.03 leave its meals alone (about 105), at 0.1 cost up to half, at 0.3 two thirds, at 1 all. MAP-Elites' random candidates use scales 0.1–3 and its mutations 0.005–0.5, so almost every random candidate, and many offspring, was a robot that didn't eat. Also: for a forager that doesn't learn, the discriminate score is mostly noise (the same seed scored −3.7 to +2.8 with only rounding changed), while its meals are steady.
- **A target-rich world doesn't make random rules taste: most don't move.** `World.blocks` sets the blocks of each colour (normally 4). In discriminate lives, 16 random rules (MAP-Elites' random start) tasted 20 or more blocks: none of 16 with 4 or 20 blocks of each colour, 2 of 16 with 40 (most 0). The cause is not distance: 13 of the 16 fire their thrusters 7 times or fewer in a life, then their sheets settle to rest despite the sensor pulses. The 2 that kept moving tasted 13 and 25 times in the dense world. So density helps a robot that moves; whether it moves is the first hurdle, and a random rule rarely clears it. Since a robot that doesn't move can't taste, "has it stopped moving?" is a safe, cheap screen for hopeless candidates (README, "Next goal").
- **Tagged `exp/robot-curriculum`:** the code and kernels at the end of this robot-curriculum work.

## Designing a seed rule by hand (bare-sheet experiments)

The question: what homogeneous cell rule lets useful circuits form? The hand-built gates were all geometry (diodes from narrow-to-wide paths, AND from thin paths merging into a wide one, crossings and NOT from refractoriness), so the aim is a uniform rule whose slow variable shapes conducting paths, and whose fast variables run an excitable medium along them. Built step by step within the 4-variable quadratic cell, on a bare 48×48 sheet (experiments kept in `~/neuron2025-parked/seed_design/`):
- **Excitable cells:** v grows above a threshold and decays below it (A(v² − θv), with the clamp at 1 as the upper limit: quadratic, since FitzHugh–Nagumo's cubic isn't available), and recovery w ends spikes. Recovery must be shunting (−B·v·w, not −B·w): otherwise v is driven below zero, w goes negative and re-excites (rebound spikes, the medium oscillates). Coupling must be excitatory input only (D·m·nv): with diffusive coupling m·(nv − v) a lone active cell drains before it lights its neighbours. Result: one clean wave per kick where conductance is high, none where it's low.
- **Canalisation by co-activity alone makes rings, not paths.** Strengthening m where a cell and its neighbours fire together (v·nv) strengthens whole wavefronts: excitable activity moves as fronts, not flows, so there is no source-to-sink current to carve channels (as auxin carves leaf veins).
- **Channels by anti-diffusion work.** dm = γ(m − nm), clamped, makes a labyrinth of one-cell channels from noise (a Turing-like pattern). With conductance (1 + m)/2, channels (m = +1) conduct and insulators (m = −1) are inert (with conductance m itself, insulators became inhibitory and smothered the channels). A pulse covers exactly its connected channel network and nothing else.
- **A taste-gated cut is permanent but, in a labyrinth, not specific.** dm += η·w·t (w, the recovery variable, doubles as "I fired recently"; t is taste) flips recently active channel cells to insulator after a bad taste, and the anti-diffusion holds them there. But a connected labyrinth floods: whichever input fires, every network cell has recently fired, so the cut can't tell red from blue.
- **Taste can't be carried far.** Spreading conserves it: one kick diluted over a radius of 6 cells is about 0.01. It must arrive near the colour information.
- **Colour-specific learning from one bad taste.** With taste delivered at the facing group's two input cells, and the labyrinth biased towards insulating (a constant in dm, so it breaks into short pieces), a bad taste after red's pulse cut red's channel and spared blue's in 9 of 11 sensor groups (bias 0.6, η 300), never both. Specificity needs local, non-flooding activity.
- **The tension:** the fragmentation that makes learning specific makes channels short (at most about 13 cells), too short to carry signals across the disc to the far thrusters. Approach needs long routes; specific learning needs local activity near the inputs.
- **In the robot, the hand-designed rule doesn't move** (`kernels/hand_seed.json`): about 5 thruster firings and 0 meals per life, 0.1 spikes per cell per 100 time units, and a discriminate score of 0.00, the same as doing nothing. Its short channels never carry a sensor pulse to a thruster.
- **Why evolution didn't find it:**
  - **Our scoring wouldn't keep it.** It learns but doesn't eat, so it scores like doing nothing; learning only pays in a robot that already eats.
  - **Its ingredients are worthless alone.** An excitable medium, insulated channels, a "fired recently" trace, taste at the inputs and a strong taste-gated cut give nothing until all are there (channels without learning just reduce foraging), so there's no stepping stone.
  - **It's far away.** Its coefficients are large and specific (the cut is 300), while the searches take small steps (0.005–0.5) and penalise large coefficients.
  - **It's a different kind of dynamics.** Evolved rules are busy, oscillating media; this is a quiet excitable one, and the curriculum (move, approach, graze rewarding motion and meals) digs deeper into busy-forager territory.
  - Discriminate on 24×24 with taste at the inputs (10 hours, 33,000 candidates, 197 niches) found no colour learning either; best validated −0.02.

## Capability experiments

A series of "can a uniform sheet do X?" experiments, done one at a time: for each, find the simplest mechanism, test it against noise, random update times, tick size and scale, and record the pros, cons and dead ends here. The aim, once enough capabilities work, is to compose them: lay down a body plan, grow paths between the parts it defines, then learn on those paths. So far: growing wiring (`paths.py`) and knowing where you are (`places.py`), and before them the hand-designed excitable medium (`old_manual_gates/`, details in its own `LESSONS.md`), whose results count as capability experiments too:
- **Signal capabilities, with wiring drawn by hand:** in a medium of one shared rule (an excitable cell: a flame that fires and fuel that recovers; one dynamic variable is enough if it is a phase), with one parameter set derived from first principles, hand-drawn patterns of enabled cells give propagation along wires, one-way gates (diodes, from narrow-to-wide geometry), AND, OR and XOR, crossings (from refractoriness), oscillators and inhibition. All of it rests on threshold excitation, refractoriness and local coupling, with no clock; the price is races and timing windows.
- **Routing learned by reward:** on random sheets, with "adaptation grows routes, then reward with a temperature selects them", which of two inputs drives an output was learned and switched on 10 of 14 sheets (`twophase.py`); credit traced back from the output through the causes of its firing, and a local thermostat for exploration.
- **Not achieved there:** AND by learning (crediting one thread grows routes; AND needs every contributor credited, and each fix for one broke the other; random-order training killed routes for good), and learning in the robot (sparse, rare reward and floods; the firing cost wore the sheet below its reflex).
- **What they share with the new experiments:** the wiring there was given (the "enabled" map), and is now what `paths.py` grows; the logic and crossings there are capabilities a grown wiring will need.

## Growing wiring

- **Cells or variables: the same trade-off, paid in different places.** A block of simple cells can do what one richer cell does (coarse-graining, Rule 110's meta-blocks), and both get past the limits of 2D: variables by layering (a pulse in w passes under one in v), cells by gliders and timing. But the search pays differently: a bigger sheet leaves the rule's search space as it is (it only costs compute), while each variable makes it much larger (180 coefficients with four variables, 882 with six and cubics).
- **The real question is where the information lives.** The hand-designed medium was not really one variable on a big sheet: its function lived in the "enabled" map, wiring drawn from outside, information the rule never produced. A uniform sheet starting from noise has none, so all structure has to be grown by the rule (development) and then changed by experience (learning). Excitation plus grown wiring is three variables at least (v, w and a structural m), as the hand-designed seed needed. A bigger sheet helps only if the rule grows useful structure in it (48×48 gave room, and nothing new appeared).
- **The polynomial cell searches over all physics, not within one.** Every coefficient of a general polynomial is free, so the search ranges over excitable media, oscillators, diffusion and endless rules that go quiet or flood (most random rules don't even keep the robot moving). The alternative is to fix the form of the physics by hand (excitation, grown conductance, a taste-gated change) and evolve only its few knobs: 10–20 numbers, which CMA-ES searches well, and a rule that can be said in a sentence. Choosing the physics is allowed help in finding the creature; if the family fails, that is a clear result too.
- **Anti-diffusion makes a texture, not routing.** dm = γ(m − nm) grows the finest pattern the grid allows (alternate cells), because nothing in it knows any longer distance: it can't make paths 3 or 10 cells apart, and it fills the whole sheet with wiring whether used or not. Spacing can be set by two chemicals (Turing's activator and inhibitor, as in the Gierer–Meinhardt model: a slowly spreading activator that boosts itself and a fast-spreading inhibitor it produces, spacing set by the inhibitor's reach, as in zebra stripes and hair follicles), but a regular pattern is still the same everywhere, whatever the sensors and thrusters need.
- **Purposeful paths come from flow, plus inhibition for spacing.** Where something conserved flows from sources to sinks, cells that carry more of it get better at carrying it and take flow from their neighbours, until broad flow narrows to paths: leaf veins (canalisation of auxin), slime-mould tubes (Physarum; flow reinforcing faster than in proportion leaves a single path, Tero et al. 2007), blood vessels whose width follows their flow (Murray's law). Paths form only where flow is needed, as wide as the load, and an established path inhibiting new ones near it sets their spacing (the patches between leaf veins). Our hand-designed rings were the same mechanism without a flow: excitation moves as fronts, not from a source to a sink, so reinforcing co-activity made rings.
- **Two routes to a sparse network, as in nature.** Start dense and prune (slime mould, canalisation), or grow paths through a quiet medium up gradients and then refine them by activity (axon guidance, then activity-dependent pruning; the retina even makes its own spontaneous waves to refine its wiring before the eyes open). Nervous systems do both, in that order. Reopening growth and pruning when something new must be learned resembles critical periods reopening (see "Recruiting latent capability", below).
- **A gradient gives the direction; one chemical is enough.** A chemical poured in at a source and drained at a sink, spreading by averaging, settles into a gradient whose steepest lines run from one to the other: following the falling gradient from the source leads to the sink. Separate chemicals for the two ends (one falling while the other rises) give the same lines, but a chemical per end doesn't scale, and identical cells can't know which variable belongs to which end; one shared chemical, or one per kind of end (as growth cones follow one cue per kind of target), avoids that. A growing tip following the gradient has to pick a direction, which mean-input cells can't sense directly; pruning from dense doesn't need a tip, because the whole sheet carries the flow along those same lines and the strongest line wins.
- **Cells can't sense flow yet.** A cell sees its neighbours' mean, and in a smooth gradient that equals its own value, so it can't tell that a strong flow passes through it. The mean of the squared differences from its neighbours, mean of (c_neighbour − c)², is large where c is steep in any direction: a flow signal that keeps cells symmetric and blind to position, far smaller than Sobel gradient inputs.
- **What "sparse" should cost.** If most cells are to end up completely inactive, activity itself should cost, not v's crossings of the firing level (the current cost, under which a cell held high is free).

- **The sink is what draws the wiring.** A source alone makes a blob that fills up and stops flowing; draining at the sink is what creates flow, and flow is what wires. So wiring is drawn by demand, not pushed out by supply: the sink decides where paths go. Two sinks would compete for wiring, the harder drain winning, and learning could be a change in how hard a sink drains (a thruster whose firing was followed by a good taste pulling harder, so the wiring reroutes to it). In development, target cells release growth factors that the neurons wired to them depend on (the neurotrophic hypothesis).
- **A path grows across a bare sheet (`paths.py`), by hand-chosen physics, no evolution.** A 48×48 sheet; a chemical c kicked in pulses at a source by one edge and drained at a sink by the other, spreading through conductance g (between two cells in proportion to both their g); g grows with the flow through the cell (g × root mean square difference of c from its neighbours, no sense of direction) faster than in proportion (power 3, half-rate at a flow HALF), decays slowly, and starts at about 1 everywhere. Findings:
  - **Timescales come first.** With g changing as fast as c spreads (decay 0.01 per unit time), every cell's g collapsed before c had crossed the sheet (about 3,000 time units). g has to be much slower (decay 0.0001).
  - **The threshold sets the regime.** At HALF 0.005 nothing prunes (97% conducting); at 0.02 about half; at 0.04–0.05 a thick "comet" joins source and sink (16–23% conducting, thick at the source, where flow spreads in all directions, narrowing to the sink: width follows load). The comet held with another seed and at tick sizes 0.25, 0.5 and 1.
  - **Stronger competition grows a tree, but it can be cut off.** At HALF 0.08 and above the dense sheet collapses to a branching tree growing out of the source, like lightning, along the grid's diagonals (the 8-neighbour mean treats diagonal neighbours as near as the others). But the cells round the sink lost their conductance before the chemical reached them, so the sink was walled off and couldn't pull, and the tree grew blind and never joined.
  - **A floor on conductance fixes it.** With g never below 0.1, a cell that has stopped conducting still leaks a little, so the sink's pull is felt across the sheet. The tree then reaches the sink: two branches arrive, and the sheet settles into a loop of two paths from source to sink (about 14.5% of cells conducting), with the same result at another seed and at tick size 0.25. Neither branch wins: the competition doesn't yet pick one route, and the paths are several cells wide. The floor is also what would let wiring regrow when needed.
  - **Weighting diagonal neighbours a quarter (the isotropic choice) removed the diagonal bias.** With all 8 neighbours averaged equally, a diagonal neighbour (√2 away) counts as much as one beside, so c spreads further along the diagonals, gradients are square-ish, tips grow along 45° lines, and a zig-zag route is as short as a straight one in the medium's own geometry. Weighted, the loop came out round and the diagonal spikes mostly went. The weights belong to the sheet (who neighbours whom, and how much), not the cell; a hexagonal grid would get round space without weights.
  - **Growth was saturated, so nothing competed.** In the loop the cells carried about 3× the half-rate flow, so every cell of both routes, and across each path's width, grew at about 95% of the full rate. Raising the threshold so path cells sit mid-curve makes splitting the flow costly: at HALF 0.15 the loop forms and one side then fades; at HALF 0.25 a single thin line grows from the source, tip first, and reaches the sink (about 3.3% of cells conducting after 300,000 time units), at two seeds and tick sizes 0.25 and 0.5. There is no dense phase: the same physics now grows a path up the gradient (the second route). It isn't always straight (seed 1 made a gentle arc): whichever branch gets going first wins.
  - **A harder drain didn't win the path.** With two sinks (rows 12 and 36 of the left edge), the line grew towards the gap between them and then turned to the lower sink, the same whether the upper one drained 1×, 2× or 10× as hard (at 10× a short stub grew from the upper sink, but never joined). Probably the drain isn't the bottleneck: getting c to a sink through the floor-conductance cells round it is (two floor cells pass 0.01 between them). Drain strength is its own question, separate from forming a path.
  - **Moved ends are rejoined by reusing the old path, not by a new direct one.** Once the path had formed (seed 0, by about 250,000 time units), at 300,000 the sink or the source jumped from row 24 to row 8 of its edge, and the life ran to 900,000. Moving the sink: the old end segment faded and the path's last stretch re-grew, bending up to the new sink; most of the old path stayed (joined, 46 steps, 3.8% of cells). Moving the source: the new source grew a short connector down to the old path, which carried on to the sink unchanged (joined, but 54 steps, 1.26× the straight route, 4.5% of cells), and it didn't straighten by the end. So the wiring adapts by local repair, keeping what exists: a form of memory (hysteresis), economical in growth but not optimal in length. Nothing pushes it to straighten once joined: every cell of a single path carries the same flow whatever its length.
  - The "path length" measure counts steps between 8-connected cells, so a curved route can still read as 1.00× straight; it says the two ends are joined, not that the route is direct.

- **Why the flow rule is slow, and what's fast.** Two causes. Spreading by averaging is a random walk: distance grows with the square root of time, so a usable gradient across 48 cells takes thousands of ticks, and twice the width four times as long. Worse, the wiring was made slow (a cell takes about 20,000 ticks to switch) and grows tip first, so the time adds up cell by cell along the path: about 600,000 ticks per path. Simply speeding the wiring up 3–100× broke it again (the sheet round the source went dark before the chemical spread, and the tip crept through dark cells). Compiling the update (Numba) gave 3.5× in real time, the same result. What's fast is relay rather than averaging: a signal passed on at full strength crosses the sheet in about one tick per cell.
- **The minimum of the neighbours is as allowable as their mean** (it is just as symmetric: no direction, no position). With it, a cell can hold its distance to the nearest sink, dk: a sink holds 0, every other cell takes the smallest of its neighbours' dk plus the step to that neighbour. That settles in about one tick per cell of distance, is exact, needs no clock, and repairs itself when the sink moves. It is how routers find routes (distance-vector routing), and the wavefront idea of Lee's algorithm for routing circuit boards. One map per kind of end, not per end, so it scales to many ends.
- **The path follows the falling distance from the source (the hops rule, `paths.py --rule hops`).** The source is on the path; a cell joins when a neighbour on the path has it as its best next step towards the sink (each cell shows the best it could reach next, its neighbours' smallest dk plus the step; the input is the largest, over neighbours on the path, of that minus the step). Recomputed every tick, so cells no longer on the way drop out. Result: a single path one cell wide (1.9–2.0% of cells) in under 100 ticks (about 50 for the map, 45 for the path), against about 600,000 for the flow rule; the same at tick size 0.5 and another seed. When the sink or the source moves, the old path vanishes and a new direct one forms to the new end within about 100 ticks: replacement, not the flow rule's local repair. With two sinks the path goes to the nearer one.
- **Symmetry has to be broken, and noise breaks it.** On a grid the minimum rule measures distance in steps, and a route of 16 diagonal and 27 straight steps is equally short in any order, so a whole parallelogram of cells lay on some shortest route and lit up as path (two sinks gave a chevron). Perfectly identical cells in a perfectly regular sheet can't choose between equal options. Giving each cell a fixed roughness from birth (its crossing cost 1 to 1.5, local, like the starting noise in every life) makes one route strictly shortest, and the path one cell wide. The same principle runs through the project: lives start from noise so structure can form (stripes, the circuits on 24×24), Turing patterns and the flow rule's competition amplify small differences, and real tissue relies on cell-to-cell variation (Notch–Delta lateral inhibition picks which of two identical cells becomes which). Ties between equal choices are the norm in a uniform medium; something local and random must break them, and the rule then has to make the choice stick.
- **Every rule must be robust to noise, and to cells not updating in lockstep** (the lizard NCA skipped each cell's update with probability 0.5). In the hops rule: pulsed ends (source and sink cells kicked at random times on two input channels, each kick topping up a fading hold, so the cell, not the world, holds the level) and asynchronous updates (each cell updating half the ticks) both kept a single thin path, at tick sizes 1 and 0.5. Jitter of 0.01 in the distances broke it completely: a path cell was on only while its predecessor's check passed that very tick, so the path was a chain of 44 links that all had to pass at once, and with noise the best next step between near-equal neighbours flickered. Hysteresis fixed the breaking (a cell joins at once and leaves slowly): joined 100% of the time with jitter 0.01–0.03, asynchronous or not, at both tick sizes. But the path became a braid of near-equal routes (60–450 cells instead of 44), and after a move a fan (18% of cells), because the slow fade keeps every route that was briefly best. What's missing is competition between neighbouring path cells, which the flow rule had. Noisy and asynchronous is now the default (UPDATE 0.5, JITTER 0.01).
- **A moved sink leaves a dying valley ("count to infinity").** Moving the sink in the noisy hops rule made a burst of path cells round the old sink that grew for a while, then faded. Traced: (1) the old sink cell's hold (topped up to about 10 by its kicks, fading 5% per time unit) kept it a sink for about 55 ticks after its kicks stopped; (2) then its distance could only climb as fast as its neighbours', which were low only because of it, so the neighbourhood propped itself up and rose together a step at a time (0 to 34 over about 135 ticks, slower for skipping half its updates). While it climbed it was still the lowest place around, so paths ran into it, and its nearly flat floor gave many near-ties that the jitter and loose match marked as path (27 path cells near it rising to 78); once it reached the level set by the new sink, they faded. It is the classic flaw of distance-vector routing (good news spreads fast, bad news slowly), fixed there by route poisoning (a vanished destination announced as infinitely far) or hold-downs. A local version: a cell whose distance would rise jumps to unknown (FAR) and rebuilds from fresh neighbours.
- **Shortest-to-nearest was a side effect, not a goal.** With one map for all sinks, each source only ever sees its nearest sink. For a robot, which sensor drives which thruster is what has to be learned, so it mustn't be settled by geometry first. The goal became one connected network joining every source and every sink, with junctions where paths converge and diverge (close to a Steiner tree, as roads and rivers): anatomy grown by development, and learning at the junctions (how much of a signal goes down each branch, changed by a three-factor rule), as brains over-connect and then strengthen or prune by experience.
- **Growing a network (`paths.py`, rewritten around the hops rule; the flow rule is at tag `exp/paths-flow`).** Two maps, distance to the nearest source and to the nearest sink; paths grow from sources down the sink map and from sinks down the source map; a path cell links back to its end along the path (its path neighbours' smallest hop count plus 1), and a part of the network linked to a sink anchors the sink map at 0, so it attracts the next path like a sink: paths merge, making junctions. Three things had to be fixed on the way. (1) Anchored network kept "growing" sideways by accidents of roughness, and anything that joined became linked and stayed: the network flooded (57–78% of the sheet). Only tips still growing may extend: towards the sinks only from path linked to a source and not yet to a sink, and the other way round. (2) A cell that had only just joined, linked to nothing for one tick, grew down both maps, sprouting a fishbone. Same fix. (3) A neighbour's "best next step" is a tick behind, so in a landscape still moving (the sink map is anchored on the sink's own path as it grows) nothing ever matched exactly, and a loose match made blobs: each cell keeps its previous distances, which its neighbours' best was worked out from, and is compared with those. Result without noise: thin paths with Y-shaped junctions (a source's path joining another's network), but ends pair off into separate networks (3 sources and 2 sinks: two networks, 3 of 6 pairs joined), since a network linked both ways no longer grows, and can't tell another network from itself. With noise and asynchronous updates everything joined, but as blobs (20–32% of the sheet), since the comparison with a neighbour's best step still needs a tolerance.
- **Poisoning cures count to infinity.** A distance that would grow by more than half a step, or a link to an end along the path that would get longer, becomes unknown instead and is rebuilt from fresh neighbours. Without it, a network outlived its sink by hundreds of ticks (the cells round a removed sink kept vouching for each other, rising a hop at a time up to the cap). With it, removing the sink of a separate network killed that network within about 100 ticks (most of it the sink's hold fading), and its source reconnected to the remaining network. In the noisy run removing a sink changed nothing, but for another reason: everything had merged into one blob still linked to a source and the remaining sink, and any cell linked both ways stays.
- **Following a neighbour's best next step is brittle under noise.** It depends on numbers worked out by different cells at different moments agreeing within a tolerance: too tight and nothing grows, too loose and paths thicken into blobs. Forbidding joining sideways (a cell may join by growth only if at most two neighbours are path already, a symmetric count) removed the blobs (46 cells for one pair, noisy) but the noisy path then broke into fragments that never joined, and the clean network lost a link (2 of 6 pairs instead of 3), so it was taken out. A path that holds under noise needs a choice that doesn't rest on exact agreement between cells.
- **Choose by name, only at the tip, and stop vouching blindly.** Three changes made networks thin and responsive. (1) Each cell is born with a random tag (nothing positional); a growing path's tip announces the tag of its best next neighbour, and a cell joins only if a path neighbour announces its tag: an exact match, so noise changes which cell is chosen but never lets two in (comparing distances within a tolerance did, making blobs). (2) Only the tip chooses freely; a cell behind it chooses among path cells only, which keeps the chain alive without sprouting side branches (noise in the middle of a path did). (3) A path cell linked both ways no longer stays just for being linked (blobs became permanent that way): it stays while it is on a through-route, its hops to a source plus hops to a sink no higher than some linked path neighbour's, so dead-end stubs wither from the tip back. With cells updating at random times and no jitter: 3 sources and 2 sinks joined 6 of 6 pairs (before: 3 of 6, in two separate networks), and after a sink was removed or moved, the network re-joined (3 of 3, 1 of 1). Leftovers pile up somewhat after changes (one pair: 58 path cells, 102 after a move), and with jitter only 3 of 6 pairs joined.
- **Lockstep makes poisoning oscillate.** With every cell updating every tick, after a change the distance map flickered for good in a period-2 checkerboard: a cell whose neighbours had just gone unknown saw its best option worsen and poisoned itself, while they recovered from it, and next tick they swapped. Updating at random times (as the lizard NCA does) breaks the lockstep, so it is no longer optional; `--clean` now only turns the jitter off.
- **With no sink at all, stale values lived on, and paths erupted towards them.** Removing every sink left 2,000 cells with a "known" sink distance and hundreds of cells "linked to a sink" hundreds of ticks later, and sources kept growing paths towards the stale lows. Cause: a poisoned cell at once re-adopted a neighbour's stale value (it looks like an improvement on unknown), so the stale cluster just climbed together, a hop every few ticks, towards the cap (136 of 200 at tick 1,000). A lower cap only shortens it. A refractory period after poisoning (the cell takes nothing from its neighbours for a while, so the loss spreads through everything that relied on it before anything stale can be re-adopted; in routing, a hold-down) cleaned up: with all sinks gone, the path fell to 3–14 cells. But it hurt joining: 3 sources and 2 sinks joined only 1–2 of 6 pairs (6 of 6 without it), and a moved sink re-joined only with the longest period tried (60), because the ordinary wobbles of a network still forming also look like "my value got worse" and make cells refractory. Patching the min rule has hit diminishing returns: each fix of count-to-infinity costs stability somewhere else.
- **Breadcrumbs: the two tricks that make path-making simple and robust** (`paths.py`, the wave rule). (1) Sinks send out waves: a sink kick excites the sink, and each cell next to an excited one becomes excited in turn, then rests (refractory), so waves only travel outwards and fade at the edges; the wave counts its steps. (2) Each cell remembers which neighbour the wave came from (its breadcrumb, by that neighbour's tag); a source is on the path, and so is any cell a path cell's breadcrumb points to, so a path is the trail of breadcrumbs from a source to the nearest sink. Everything else is subtext: a breadcrumb is kept unless the wave now comes clearly shorter (10%) another way; a cell forgets everything when no wave has come for a while; ends are pulsed and hold their state. No distances are compared between cells over time, nothing stale can circulate (all information comes fresh from a real sink), and breadcrumbs can't loop (a kept breadcrumb points to a cell the same wave reached first, with a smaller count). Tested on 4 seeds and tick sizes 1 and 0.5, with jitter: every source joined a sink in every scene; after a sink moved, the path re-formed along the new shortest route within 150 ticks; after a sink was removed, the stranded sources re-routed to the remaining one; with all sinks removed every path vanished once the memory expired. Single paths are exactly as long as a straight line (44 cells on 48×48, 92 on 96×96); several sources share branches into the sinks (6 sources, 3 sinks on 96×96: 169 cells). Robust to jitter 20 times the default and to any update rate whose windows still span enough updates (fails at a quarter of ticks).
- **The wave must count its steps; arrival order won't do.** A count-free version ("remember which neighbour woke you first", judged by how long before you each neighbour was woken) joined only about half the time: with random update times, which of two neighbours at nearly the same distance wakes first is close to a coin toss, so breadcrumbs kept switching and chains were cut. The count a wave carries doesn't depend on when cells happened to update, so it survives the randomness. (Comparing "time left excited" across moments was a further trap: it shrinks for everyone, so it must be taken relative to one's own.)
- **How the breadcrumb rule was found (dead ends included).** Waves first gave distances 2–3× too large: with random update times a cell sometimes misses a wave and later re-excites cells that have recovered (re-entry, as in heart arrhythmia); fixed by excitation long enough that a cell rarely misses it (16 time units) and refractoriness long enough that a late one finds everyone resting (40). Following the falling distance ("downhill") failed: wave distances carry a few percent of noise unevenly, so the map has pits where a chain gets stuck; breadcrumbs can't get stuck, since they point back along the wave. Choosing the breadcrumb only at the moment of excitation failed: with random update times a cell is excited before its own parent 28% of the time; it has to watch for the whole excited window and decide when the wave has passed. Keeping a breadcrumb by comparing remembered distances made paths meander after a move; comparing ways within the same wave (consistent values) fixed that. A slow fade on path cells bridged breadcrumb switches while they were frequent, but left dead chains unravelling over hundreds of ticks; once switches were rare it was dropped (paths now go at once). Roughness from birth, needed earlier to break ties between equally short routes, turned out unnecessary: the breadcrumbs and their stickiness break ties themselves. Growing paths from both kinds of end doubled the cells and drew two different routes; growing only from sources, along the sinks' breadcrumbs, is simpler and enough.
- **What the robot needs, and nature's ways of wiring it.** Approach (Braitenberg's vehicles) needs each sensor to drive the thruster on the far side, since thrusters push away from their side: crossed wiring, so twelve inputs' paths all cross at the centre. Nature: axons find targets by matching molecular tags (Sperry's chemoaffinity), mostly through smooth gradients rather than unique labels (graded Eph receptors on retinal axons against graded ephrins in the midbrain); unique identities exist (fly Dscam's ~38,000 variants, vertebrate protocadherins) but serve self-avoidance; crossing the midline is common (commissural axons drawn by netrin, then repelled by Slit/Robo so they can't recross); later axons follow pioneers (fasciculation).
- **Ways to cross paths, measured.** (1) A separate set of variables per target (one wave and breadcrumb set per thruster): always works, as crossing paths simply don't see each other; on the robot-like ring all 12 crossed inputs joined their opposite thruster (2 seeds, about 310 cells, about 40 shared by two paths), at 6 times the variables. Diagonal paths on a grid even cross without sharing a cell. (2) One shared wave medium with labelled waves (breadcrumbs per label): fails, because waves from different sinks cancel where they meet, so a source never receives its target's wave if another sink is nearer; with rare, irregular firing two crossing paths worked part of the time, six almost never. (3) Two layers with crossings (vias) and (4) a gradient the sources aim along (ephrin-like) are untried. (5) Change the body so nothing need cross: if thrusters pulled towards their own side, each input's nearest thruster is the one it should drive, and one channel wires all 12 inputs (50 cells, 2 seeds). For a hand-built growth rule that is the simplest by far; it was ruled out only to stop evolution finding trivial wiring.
- **Cuts and walls.** A cell that can't be on a path (a cut) cuts the breadcrumb trail for good: what lies beyond it vanishes and stays gone, a ready-made 1-bit memory. A cell waves can't pass (a wall) makes the path re-route round it, within about 100 ticks.
- **One bit learned on a grown path (bare sheet).** A red and a blue input side by side, wired by the breadcrumb rule to one thruster; pulses run along the path (each path cell passes a pulse to the cell its breadcrumb names); each input keeps a trace of its own recent pulses; visits in random order (pulses at one colour's input, then a taste at both inputs, bad after the poison colour); a bad taste while an input's trace is up closes its gate (a cut at the path's start). In 4 of 4 seeds the poison colour's pulses stopped reaching the thruster after its first (or, once, second) visit while the other colour's kept arriving, and the same with the colours swapped; with no learning both kept arriving. The gate has to be at the input: paths from the two inputs merge into a shared trunk, so a cut anywhere further on would silence both, and the input is where taste already arrives in the robot. It is the three-factor rule (pulse x recent x taste), at the input; the path only routes.
- **Crossing, tested exhaustively by pulses (robot-like ring: 6 groups, a red and a blue input each, a thruster 3 cells in; each input's pulses should reach the opposite thruster and no other; experiments kept in `~/neuron2025-parked/paths_experiments/cross/`).** The main lesson: crossing itself is free (pulses following breadcrumbs in separate channels don't interfere even on shared cells, and diagonal paths cross at an X without sharing a cell); what costs is routing to a particular target that isn't the nearest. Each mechanism is a different answer to "how does a pulse know which far target to head for?":
  1. **A map per target** (one breadcrumb channel per thruster): 100% correct, no crosstalk, 3 seeds. Cost grows with targets (6 sets of wave variables). Simplest to state; most expensive.
  2. **Make the right target the nearest** (pulling thrusters: uncrossed wiring): 100%, one channel, 50 cells for all 12 inputs. A change of body, not of cell; by far the simplest.
  3. **Rays: fly straight** (a pulse leaves inwards up the distance-from-edge gradient, carries its direction across, then follows breadcrumbs to the nearest thruster at the far edge): 100% with no noise, but dead reckoning accumulates error: a direction wobble of 3° per step gave 63–68% correct, 6° about 50%. Re-aiming at the centre on the way in made it worse (67% even without noise): at the centre the gradient flattens, so the direction leaving it is arbitrary. Free crossing, no wiring, but fragile.
  4. **A hub (chiasm): all paths meet at the centre, and pulses go straight through** (inputs grow paths to a central sink in one channel, thrusters in another; a pulse comes in on the first, carrying a heading, and goes out on the second against the breadcrumbs, choosing at each branch the way that keeps its heading): 83–100% (mean 92% with a long heading memory, 6 seeds), for a fixed two channels however many paths. With a single shared channel most pulses were lost into the far group's input branches. Errors come from paths wiggling as they enter the hub, blurring "straight on".
  5. **Addresses (landmarks, like GPS)**: four landmark cells on the rim send waves; differences of two counts give coordinates; a pulse carries its input's coordinates reflected through the centre and steps to the neighbour nearest that address, then follows breadcrumbs to the nearest thruster. A fixed five channels however many targets; closed-loop, so errors don't accumulate; general (any target, not just opposite). With exact distances 92% (one geometric corner case), but the wave counts' few-percent errors, doubled by taking differences, bend the coordinates: 25–83% at first; letting pulses cross small plateaus and reading the maps between waves, averaged over several, brought it to 75–100% (mean about 88%).
  6. **Shared medium with labelled waves**: fails (waves from different sinks cancel where they meet), as recorded above.
  7. **Untried, by reasoning**: two layers with vias and per-cell slots for several breadcrumbs solve crosstalk, which isn't the problem, not routing, which is; a "go to the farthest sink" rule would route crossed wiring with one map, but the farthest-sink distance has no simple local recurrence like the nearest's (and colliding waves erase it).
  Verdict, by simplicity then robustness: change the body (2), else a map per target (1), else the hub (4) or addresses (5) at fixed cost with about 90% accuracy; rays (3) only in a noise-free world.
- **Paths that persist, quietly: two more tricks** (`paths.py`, PERSIST). (3) A sink calls (sends waves) only while hungry, when no pulse has reached it for a while; pulses run along paths (a source kick sends one; each path cell passes it to the cell its breadcrumb names), so once a path delivers, its sink stops calling and the sheet goes quiet. (4) A path lives as long as its sink thanks it: a sink receiving a pulse sends an acknowledgement back up the path, refreshing the breadcrumbs as waves do, like the growth factor a neuron's target sends back along the axon (the neurotrophic hypothesis). A path to a vanished sink is no longer thanked and is forgotten. Results (4 seeds, tick sizes 1 and 0.5, noise): once wired, wave activity falls to zero (from 0.28 of cells excited at any moment without persistence) and paths hold indefinitely; a removed sink's paths clear; damage across a path breaks it, its sink gets hungry and calls, and the path re-routes round the damage, then the sheet goes quiet again; a moved sink is re-wired; every scene ends fully joined 600 ticks after a change. The price is reaction time: a stranded source takes 300–600 ticks to be re-wired, against about 150 without persistence, and forgetting a removed sink about 350.
- **What it took (persistence).** "Pulses refresh the path" would keep a path to a dead sink alive (pulses still run along it), hence acknowledgements from the sink instead. Pulses died where they bunched up (random update times delay them unevenly, and a pulse reaching a cell still resting from the last one dies): a short rest after passing (2) is enough, since breadcrumbs already stop pulses going backwards. A memory of 100 broke paths now and then on a rare long gap in thanks; 200 didn't. A fed sink stops calling, so sources not yet wired, or stranded when their sink went, could never be wired: "fed sinks still wave now and then" spoiled the quiet (even rare waves keep cells excited for long), so instead a source not thanked for a while sends a bare request wave, and a sink it reaches calls for a while, fed or not (an answer cut short by the next pulse from a source it already served left the latecomer unwired). Then a sink no source wanted, hungry for ever and alone in calling, swept the sheet with waves that re-pointed everyone's breadcrumbs to itself, hijacking working paths; so a cell thanked recently keeps its breadcrumb whatever waves pass (if it works, don't fix it). Finally, loneliness and hunger thresholds of 200 (rather than 100) stopped occasional false alarms, leaving the wired sheet silent.
- **A first wrong test, noted:** marking a cell as path when its ds + dk (distances to source and to sink) is no larger than its neighbours' smallest also marks countless fragments: every cell lies on its own best route through it, along which ds + dk is constant. The path has to be grown from an end, or compared with the true minimum.

## Knowing where you are (`places.py`)

The task: on a square sheet of identical cells, all starting the same bar noise, the four corner cells and the centre cells (one cell, or the central four on an even size) come to identify themselves, with no global knowledge. The lizard's question in miniature: where am I in the body?

- **All positional information comes from the edge.** In a uniform sheet nothing else differs from place to place (by symmetry), so position has to be read off the boundary, as in Wolpert's "positional information" from signalling boundaries. A cell senses the edge locally: beyond it, it simply has no neighbour.
- **Counting steps (the simplest, and the most robust to scale): two tricks.** (1) Count steps in from the edge (an edge cell holds 0, every other cell its smallest neighbour's count plus one): the centre is where the count is highest, no neighbour higher. (2) The centre holds 0 in a second count, out from it: the corners are where that count is highest. Right in every run (4 seeds, tick sizes 1 and 0.5) within about 300 ticks on 48×48 and stays right; also at 24, 49 (one centre cell) and 96 (about 640 ticks: time grows with the size), and with cells updating a quarter of ticks.
- **Count out from the centre as a rook moves.** With diagonal steps of √2 (round space), the outward count climbs only 0.41 per cell along an edge towards a corner, too little above the noise, and edge middles kept claiming to be corners; with a diagonal step counting 2 (rows and columns only), it climbs a whole step per cell and the corners stand out.
- **Whole numbers resist noise.** With counts as real numbers, noise piled up along them (the minimum picks out low values): at jitter 0.01 the four tied centre cells differed by 0.08, and at 0.1 everything failed. Read as whole numbers (rounded), noise under half a step is wiped out at every step, as digital signals resist noise; and a count must jump to its target (with a chance matching the rate, so the same speed at any tick size) rather than creep, or it sits between whole numbers and rounding becomes a coin toss. Then right at jitter 0.1 in all runs (91% of the time after); at 0.2 it fails, since noise beyond half a step is then frequent and the minimum copies a single bad low value onwards.
- **A leaky chemical (one variable): simplest to state, slow.** Every cell makes a little chemical; it spreads by averaging with the eight neighbour slots, and a slot beyond the edge holds none, so it leaks away there; it settles highest at the centre and lowest at the corners (they leak on five sides). Centre: no neighbour higher; corner: clearly lower than every neighbour. It survives more noise than counting (averaging cancels noise rather than passing it on), but takes far longer (about 750 ticks on 48×48, 2,750 on 96: diffusion time grows with the square of the size) and flickers more (right 90% of the time after on 48×48, 44% with a single centre cell, whose dome top is flattest). It needed a strong production (the dome's real differences grow with it, the noise doesn't), and corners judged as clear dips: the edge middles are flat, and "no neighbour lower" let noise make false corners there.
- **Next steps for this capability:** more places than corners and centre (edge middles; the whole of a coordinate system, by breaking the remaining symmetry, e.g. picking one corner as the origin); then the body plan this gives (regions, sensor and thruster sites) as the frame for paths and learning.

## Related work

- **Neural Cellular Automata, the "lizard" (Growing NCA).** Each cell had 16 variables: RGBA plus 12 others. It saw the 16 values of each of its 8 immediate neighbours, and nothing else, and held no other internal state. Horizontal and vertical Sobel gradients, computed from the neighbours only, were inputs to the cell's small neural network. The 12 extra channels appear to encode where a cell belongs and what it should be doing, through combinations of values whose meanings vary between training runs. They are strongly correlated: one study estimated only 3–6 effective dimensions. One channel seems to indicate "edge", and another may indicate the head-tail axis. Even when the lizard looks stationary, internal activity continues, and suppressing small updates can impair repair: the stable image seems to rest on ongoing, distributed computation.
  - **The update rule** (checked against the paper, Mordvintsev et al., Distill 2020): the cell's 16 values and their 16 x- and 16 y-Sobel gradients (48 inputs) go through one hidden layer (a dense layer to 128 ReLU units, then a dense layer back to 16): about 8,000 parameters, shallow, deep only by iteration. The last layer starts at zero, so a new network does nothing at first (as our complexification starts new variables at zero). Each cell's update is skipped with probability 0.5 (no global clock), and a cell is cleared unless some cell in its 3×3 neighbourhood has alpha above 0.1. Trained by backpropagation through time over rollouts of 64–96 steps, with a pixel-wise squared-error loss.
  - **A small ReLU network may suit us better than cubic terms:** ReLUs are thresholds, which spiking and gating need (our quadratic fakes them with v² and the clamp), and 8 inputs, 16 hidden ReLUs and 4 outputs is about 210 parameters, close to our 180, with far richer nonlinearity. It would be harder to state in words, and we'd have to evolve it: our simulation isn't differentiable (spikes are counted at crossings, blocks eaten on contact), so backpropagation isn't available without a rebuild.
  - **For us:** the Sobel gradients are the gradient inputs we considered and set aside (each variable's east-minus-west and south-minus-north difference). 3–6 effective dimensions is close to our 4 variables. Ongoing activity beneath a stable surface fits "From dynamic to static memory" below, and our own sheets, which are never still.

## Ideas for future research

- **From dynamic to static memory (an idea, untested).** Even rules with no lasting state in a cell, like Life, can store state in dynamic patterns: an oscillator that is started or stopped. That costs energy for as long as it is held, like short-term memory. If an oscillation that persists gradually builds up a trace in a slow variable (in our cell, m could track a running average of local activity), the trace becomes long-term memory, and can then inhibit the oscillator, so the memory no longer costs energy. The key is that the hand-over from dynamic to static should be invisible to the rest of the network: the same information, read the same way, on a different substrate. This may be what representational drift is: in brains, the neurons representing something change over days while behaviour stays stable. It fits our scoring too: the activity cost would reward the shift, since reverberation costs spikes and a stored trace doesn't. It parallels the neuroscience of working memory as persistent activity, consolidated over time into synaptic change.
- **Add capability as the challenge grows (complexification).** Give each stage only the variables it needs, then add one for the next challenge (learning), with the earlier coefficients frozen and the new variable's starting at zero, so it changes nothing until it's useful. This is how NEAT-style evolution starts minimal, how progressive networks add capacity per task without overwriting old skills, and how gene duplication frees a copy for a new job. In our cell a rule with fewer variables is the full rule with the missing variables' coefficients held at zero, so it needs only a mask in the search. It addresses two of the reasons evolution didn't find the hand-designed rule: the search explores only the new variable, near a robot that already works.
- **Recruiting latent capability when failing (an idea).** Could a cell recruit an unused variable only when it detects it isn't solving a problem? A slow variable stays unused but is then slow to learn with. The way out is to separate the gate from the capability: a fast variable whose terms are multiplied by a slow gate, which accumulates local evidence of failure (repeated bad tastes nearby, activity never followed by reward, surprise). Precedents: the hand-designed medium's thermostat (unrewarded near misses heated cells, and only hot cells explored); metaplasticity (the plasticity of plasticity); neuromodulators of uncertainty (acetylcholine, noradrenaline) that switch on learning and exploration when the current approach fails; silent synapses and new neurons recruited on demand. In our quadratic cell a gate can scale a single variable (g·x) but not a product (g·x·y would be cubic), which limits it.
- **A learning variable and a variable that permits it (an idea).** Learning probably involves only a few cells, so it may need two variables: one that learns, and one that enables learning in the right cells, as brains keep plasticity off in most places and permit it in particular regions or critical periods. Cells could find the right places locally:
  - **distance from the edge:** a slow variable pushed up at the edge and spreading inwards gives a gradient. It needs the edge to be visible: Sobel gradients, or a sum over all 8 neighbour slots with a missing one counting as 0;
  - **closeness to sensors:** only input cells are kicked, so a slow average of local activity (spreading a little) peaks there. Since taste now arrives at the inputs, a slow average of |t| marks exactly the cells that ever taste: a plasticity zone defined by experience;
  - **a marker from the body:** input cells emitting a constant signal (allowed, but less elegant).

  Gating is three-way (zone × fired recently × taste), beyond a quadratic cell, so it needs either a cubic term or one more variable (an eligibility e built from w × zone, then m changed by e × t): three variables, each with one job. Sobel inputs on top would make the rule large (6 variables, 24 inputs: about 2,000 coefficients), searchable only by complexification (freeze what works, evolve the new variables' terms). The cell now has e and z for this, without Sobel inputs.
- **Cubic terms (now in the cell, for a cell's own variables only: 56 per variable with six variables; untested so far).** Three things the quadratic cell can only do by workaround: excitability (FitzHugh–Nagumo's v³; we use a quadratic plus the clamp), three-factor learning (pre × post × taste; we use a trace variable, w, then w·t), and a gate that switches on a product (g·x·y). Cubic terms would make each direct, but they grow fast: with v and w only, 20 more terms per variable (70 coefficients instead of 30); with all four variables, 120 more (660 instead of 180). A middle way is a few chosen cubics (each variable's own cube, or the products known to matter), at the price of choosing the physics by hand. Cubics of the neighbour means were left out (hundreds more terms); a neighbour's signal can reach a cubic through a trace variable such as e.
- **Finding the minimum number of variables (for once a learner exists).** The concept is the intrinsic (effective) dimensionality of the cells' state, or in control theory a minimal realisation: the smallest state that reproduces the same input-output behaviour.
  - **Measure:** PCA over every cell's variables across many lives (how many components carry about 95% of the variance), or nonlinear estimators (participation ratio, nearest-neighbour methods).
  - **Reduce:** rotate and truncate (model order reduction: proper orthogonal decomposition with Galerkin projection). A quadratic rule rewritten in rotated variables is still exactly quadratic, so rotate onto the principal components, drop the weak ones, and briefly re-evolve. Or: ablation (hold one variable at zero, keep it only if that hurts); group sparsity in evolution (penalise each variable as a whole, so unneeded ones go to zero together, the complement of complexification); distillation (evolve a smaller cell to imitate the larger one).
  - **A variable may matter only in some cells, and mean different things in different places.** Dopamine is the brain's example: a reward-prediction error in the nucleus accumbens, movement vigour in the dorsal striatum (lost in Parkinson's), working memory in prefrontal cortex, prolactin suppression in the pituitary. The meaning is set locally (receptor types, circuit). Our uniform rule allows the same: t is only ever kicked at the input cells, and m could mean "conducts" in the interior and "this colour was bad" near a sensor; every cell carries every variable, as every cell carries the whole genome. Machine learning calls this superposition, or polysemanticity. It defeats a whole-sheet PCA (a variable vital in a few cells has little total variance), so judge importance by ablation (hold a variable at zero in one region and see what breaks), analyse by region (inputs, thrusters, interior), and use sparse or local decompositions. A variable that stays at zero in most cells costs only compute, not behaviour.
  - **Inside the cells:** local rules can do the essentials. Oja's and Sanger's rules make units converge to the principal components of their inputs; anti-Hebbian decorrelation (Földiák) removes redundancy; a metabolic cost on holding state away from zero lets unused variables fade, the within-life version of group sparsity, akin to our activity cost.

## Speed

- **Cells as a list with neighbour lists, the rule applied one term at a time across all cells, no allocation per tick:** 3.6× faster in one process (17.5 → 5.0 ms per 2000-tick life), 2.4× on all 8 cores (185 → 448 lives per second). No swapping or major page faults in either version.
- **All 8 cores give only 2.7× one core** (540 against 203 lives per second): each life costs 4.9 ms of CPU alone and 13.8 ms with 8 running. The Core Ultra 7 266V has 4 performance and 4 low-power cores and slows its clock under full load. More workers still help, less each time (4 workers: 461 lives per second).
- **The cell step is 80–100% of a tick; the world (senses, thrusters, eating) is the rest.** Profiled with the 4-variable rule: 30 ns per cell per tick, about 13 GFLOP/s, far below what one core can do.
- **What made it faster, in order:**
  - **Lives side by side:** a genome's lives step together, each cell's lives contiguous (state is variables × (cells × lives)), so inner loops run over lives with the same neighbour pattern.
  - **Cell by cell:** gathering one cell's inputs over its lives and finishing it before the next keeps the data in the fastest cache. Term by term over all cells and lives streamed about 16 MB per tick through memory.
  - **The rule written out:** generated from TERMS as one expression per variable inside a loop over lives, with the coefficients copied into locals first. The compiler then keeps them in registers and vectorises 8 lives at a time (it couldn't while reading the coefficient array, which it couldn't prove didn't overlap the outputs). The rule's arithmetic runs at 5 ns per cell-life, near the floor of about 4 (180 multiply-adds at 16 per cycle). What remains (gathering, clamping: short loops over 64 lives) would need much more intricate code.
  - **With six variables and cubic terms** one loop body over lives holding the whole rule stopped vectorising (263 ns per cell-life, against 14 for the four-variable cell). One loop per variable, with that variable's coefficients copied into locals first, vectorises again: 36 ns, and a discriminate candidate 2.2× the four-variable cost (3.8 s against 1.7 s on one core).
- **32-bit numbers** for the cells, with their own random stream per life (so food and poison lives still pair).
  - Together: about 2× for a full evaluation (one genome: 1.45 → 0.68 s; 8 workers: 108 → 214 genomes per minute), results statistically unchanged.
- **Racing gave another ~3×:** a candidate's score is the worse over the tick sizes, so its score at dt 1 alone bounds it. Living every candidate at dt 1 first and only the few that already beat their niche's elite at dt 0.5 too costs 0.35 of a full evaluation (on the taste archive, 2% went on, and every real improvement was among them). Racing on a quarter of the worlds instead let 69% through and rejected the one improvement: improvements are rare (1 in 200), so the test must be exact, not cheap and noisy.
